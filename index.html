What they are actually testing

They said there are no right answers and that they want to see how you think, how you plan, and how you bring people with you. Take that literally. A polished plan delivered from memory scores worse than visible reasoning. What follows is not the answer. It is a map of where they planted the questions.

The core skill on the job description "Lead deep-dive gap analyses comparing global tech blueprints against local ANZ requirements, challenging proposals with data." Now look at the five contract types in the brief. Some are ANZ and EU. Some are ANZ only. That split is not decoration.

The six planted tensions, and what each one is a prompt for

Each row below is a line the case authors chose to include. Read each one as an invitation to name a tool and explain your reasoning, not as a problem to solve on the spot.

Fixed deadline, undefined scope

The Icertis expiry is a hard date. Nobody has scoped migration. Ask yourself: when time is fixed and cost is contracted, which variable is left? What technique exists for agreeing in advance what drops? How do you plan credibly when you cannot yet estimate? → Iron triangle · MoSCoW · rolling wave planning

"No one has mapped out which contracts are currently in Icertis or what state they're in"

This is the quietest sentence in the brief and possibly the loudest. Ask yourself: can anything downstream be sized, costed or sequenced before this is known? If not, what does that make it? → Critical path · data profiling · discovery as a scheduled deliverable

Vendor only builds what it is briefed

Ironclad's implementation team is capacity you do not control and cannot bank. Ask yourself: what does the signed SOW actually cover, and what does it not? Whose delay is it when requirements are late? → Scope vs SOW · change control · dependency management

Time-poor SMEs who do not report to you

Legal and Procurement own the requirements but not the project. Ask yourself: what makes an ask cheap enough for a busy person to say yes to? What is the difference between asking for input and asking for a decision? → Stakeholder engagement matrix · RACI · structured elicitation

Key ANZ Indirect user on leave for the whole of UAT

Note the word "heads up". You already know. Ask yourself: is a certainty a risk? What is it instead? And does a senior PM escalate this, or arrive with options and a recommendation? → RAID (and which letter) · UAT entry and exit criteria · decision framing

"This isn't just a technology change... people need to understand why, not just how"

They wrote this line for you. Ask yourself: if your answer is "training", how many of the five reasons a person fails to adopt a system have you addressed? → ADKAR · benefits realisation · adoption metrics

Two things not in the tension table that you should notice

There are two project managers and no stated joint forum

You are ANZ. Someone else is EU. Three of the five workflows span both. Neither of you has authority over the other. Ask yourself: where do you agree a shared design, where do you diverge, and who arbitrates when you disagree? This is the exact "global blueprint versus local market" skill in the job description, hiding inside the case.

You are "responsible for delivery". The Product Manager is "ultimately accountable for the success of the roll out"

Read those two lines from the stakeholder table together. Ask yourself: in a RACI, how many people can hold the A on one outcome? Is this an error in the brief, or a deliberate ambiguity you are expected to spot and negotiate in week one?

The trap of over-preparing They asked you not to prepare. Preparing anyway is sensible. Sounding prepared is not. If you walk in and recite a plan, you have shown recall. If you walk in able to say "the thing I would want to know first is X, because until I know it I cannot size Y", you have shown judgement. Aim for fluency with the tools, then think out loud in the room.

Build it yourself

Eleven steps, in the order a real project setup runs. Nothing here is saved automatically, so use the copy button at the bottom when you are done.

Open the two reveals in order Write your own answer first. Then open 1 · what a strong answer covers and mark your own work against the criteria. Only then open 2 · a worked answer.

That order matters. If you read the worked answer first you will judge it better than yours and try to reproduce it tomorrow from memory, which produces a recital. If you self-mark first, the worked answer lands as a correction to something you already own, which is what actually sticks.

Question → tool

Likely questions, the tools that answer each one, the shape of a strong response, and now a spoken version of each. The shape matters more than the content: notice how often the strong version names a tension, offers options with trade-offs, and ends with a recommendation rather than a request for permission.

Read the spoken versions out loud, not silently They are written the way people actually talk, with hedges, restarts and short sentences, because tomorrow is spoken and a written answer read aloud sounds rehearsed. Do not memorise them. Read one, close it, and say your own version at the wall. The gap between those two is the only thing worth practising tonight.

The question it answers

Print this or read it on the way in. Nothing here is a script. It is the minimum set of hooks that lets you find the right tool while you are talking.

The eight you must be able to wield

CharterWhat are we committing to, and who has authority to change it?
WBSWhat is all the work? Does it add to 100%, including the parts the vendor is not doing?
Iron triangleWhich constraint is fixed, and therefore which one has to give?
MoSCoWIf we run out of time, what drops, and who agreed that in advance?
RAIDWhat could derail this, who owns it, and what is the trigger for the fallback?
RACIWho decides, who does, who is consulted, who is just told?
Power/interest gridWhere do I spend my limited attention, and in what style?
ADKARWhy will people not use this, and does my plan treat that specific reason?

The distinctions that separate a senior answer from a junior one

  1. Risk vs issue vs dependency vs assumption. A certainty is not a risk. The key user's leave is known, so it is a dependency and a decision due now, not a line in the risk register.
  2. Mitigation vs contingency vs trigger. Mitigation reduces likelihood or impact now. Contingency is what you do if it lands anyway. The trigger is the signal that tells you to activate it.
  3. Severity vs priority. A cosmetic defect on the go-live screen can be low severity and high priority. They are different axes.
  4. Output vs outcome vs benefit. System is live. People use it. Contract cycle time drops. Only the third one is why anyone funded this.
  5. Vendor SOW vs project scope. The SOW covers configuration. Your scope also covers migration, training, cutover and adoption.
  6. Responsible vs accountable. Only ever one A. If the brief hands the A to two people, that is your first negotiation.
  7. Crashing vs fast-tracking. Adding resource costs money. Overlapping tasks costs risk. Neither is free, and saying which you would choose and why is the answer.

The three sentences worth having ready

  1. On the deadline: "The date is fixed and the commercial is signed, so scope and quality are the only variables I have. That means the first conversation I want with the SteerCo is about what go-live must include, not what it could include."
  2. On unknown scope: "I would not pretend to have a full plan on day one. I would plan the next four weeks in detail, put a date on when scope locks, and treat the Icertis data profiling as the deliverable that unlocks the rest of the schedule."
  3. On people: "Training only answers whether someone knows how. It does not answer whether they want to. If Legal thinks this makes their week harder, no amount of training fixes that, so I would want to understand what the change costs each group before I design the rollout."

Structure to fall back on if a question is broad

  1. Orient. What is the objective, what does done look like, who decides, what do I not know?
  2. Structure. Break the work down, map the people, name the risks, sequence it, decide what is a Must.
  3. Deliver. Build, test, migrate, cut over, support, measure, review.
  4. And throughout: govern it, communicate it, and bring people with you.

If you only remember one thing

Name the tension before you offer the tool. "These two things are in conflict, here is how I would work out which one gives" is the whole job, and it is what the brief is testing.
